How Detroit Entrepreneurs Survive the First Five Years: Lessons from Building Wright Hospitality

By Angela M. Wright, Founder, Wright Hospitality

Every time I’m in a room full of entrepreneurs talking about Detroit’s riverfront development, I end up on the edge of my seat. I know that mix of excitement and intimidation because I’ve lived it.

I opened MIX Bricktown in 2011, back when Detroit’s comeback was still a rumor. Today, Wright Hospitality operates four concepts across the city: MIX Bricktown, The LOCKEROOM Sports Lounge, Sandbox and Me & You.

But building a hospitality business takes more than opening the doors and hoping people show up. It takes resources, relationships and a plan for turning the business you build into something that lasts.

When Auntie Magazine profiled me in "Queen Among Kings," the story explored my approach to culture, business and ownership. I believe in building a brand, but I also believe in owning the ground it stands on.

That philosophy is personal. I used income from my work as DJ Lynda Carter to help fund the down payment, renovations and startup costs for my businesses and properties. In 2018, I purchased the LOCKEROOM Sports Lounge along with its building and parking lot. As Forbes reported in September 2026, I have kept the operating business and the real estate in separate entities.

For me, that is what building a business is about: creating something people love while thinking about the assets and opportunities that can sustain it.

But none of that happens if you don’t survive the first five years.

What does a small business need to survive the first five years in Detroit?

Three things: resources, support and education. Most new owners chase the first and forget the other two.

Capital opens the door. Education helps you understand how to keep the business running, while the right support system can connect you to people and opportunities you might not find on your own.

You have to be willing to learn, ask questions and take advantage of resources that can help you make better decisions. Building a business is an ongoing education, and you cannot afford to think you already know everything.

Is Detroit a good place to start a small business?

Detroit has resources that entrepreneurs should explore before trying to figure everything out on their own.

Every one of my businesses received support through Motor City Match or Motor City Re-Store. Those programs didn’t just help with build-outs and storefronts; they connected me to people who wanted to see me win.

My advice is to research the programs available to you, understand their eligibility requirements and find out how they align with your business goals. Don’t assume you have to figure out every challenge by yourself.

The resources are there to help entrepreneurs build, but you have to take the initiative to find them and put them to work.

How can you get a business loan in Detroit without perfect credit?

Start by talking to a community development financial institution, or CDFI. These lenders often work with small businesses that have difficulty accessing traditional financing, although their requirements vary.

You need a clear vision, a realistic business plan and an understanding of your finances. You should be prepared to explain what you’re building, how the business will generate revenue and how you intend to repay the loan.

Don’t wait until you’re desperate for money to start learning about your options. Build relationships with lenders, ask questions and understand what they look for in a business before you need financing.

Capital matters, but so does knowing how to use it.

Should a smaller business pursue a project like the riverfront?

Don’t let scale scare you off. Whether you’re opening your first location or operating your fourth, major developments can feel out of reach.

Here’s what I tell entrepreneurs I mentor: The size of your business today matters less than the strength of your support system and the preparation you bring to the table.

My experience has taught me to look beyond the immediate transaction. A business can generate revenue while you’re operating it, but ownership can create opportunities that extend beyond daily sales.

That’s why I think entrepreneurs should learn about commercial real estate, understand the difference between leasing and owning and consider what long-term control could mean for their businesses. Buying property isn’t the right move for every business, but understanding your options is important.

My own journey has reinforced the value of investing in what you’re building. As I shared with Forbes, income from my DJ career helped me invest in my businesses and properties. I didn’t look at that income only as money to spend. I also looked at what it could help me build.

You don’t have to start with a large portfolio to think strategically about ownership. You do have to understand your finances, evaluate opportunities carefully and build relationships with people who can help you make informed decisions.

My advice to Detroit entrepreneurs

Tap every resource Detroit offers. Apply to Motor City Match if your business qualifies. Talk to a CDFI loan officer. Take the classes. Ask questions and learn from people who have already navigated the challenges you’re facing.

And think beyond opening day. Consider what you want the business to look like five years from now, what resources you’ll need to get there and what you’re building that can last.

Then lead with heart, because people don’t come to your business for a transaction. They come to make memories.

Detroit gave me my start. The next five years of this city’s growth belong to the entrepreneurs willing to do the work, build relationships and create something that lasts.
